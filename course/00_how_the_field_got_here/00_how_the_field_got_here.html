Chapter 00 · Prologue

How the field got here

Comparative genomics did not arrive as a discipline. It arrived four times, each time because something about what could be compared changed — and each shift left behind assumptions that the next one broke.

The argument of this chapter

Four shifts, and what each one broke

History is not decoration here. Most of the field's persistent bad habits are reasonable practices from an earlier era that nobody retired. Knowing which era a method comes from tells you what it silently assumes.

ShiftWhat changedThe assumption it broke
1 · One molecule → whole genomes
1965 – 1995		 From comparing a single protein or rRNA to comparing complete gene sets That a well-chosen molecule represents the organism. Gene trees stopped agreeing with each other
2 · Pairwise → clade-scale
2002 – 2011		 From two genomes to dozens, aligned together under a tree That “conserved between A and B” is a statement about function. Power came from depth of the tree, not identity
3 · A reference → a graph
2005 – 2023		 From one linear coordinate system per species to a pangenome path through a clade That a species has a genome. Presence/absence became a property of a sample, not of a taxon
4 · Difference → evidence grade
2012 – now		 From detecting differences to grading the claim a difference supports That a significant test result is a finding. The ladder, and the reproducibility reckoning

Era by era

What each period believed, and why it was wrong

1962 – 1994

One molecule

Zuckerkandl and Pauling's molecular clock made sequence difference into time. Fitch's 1970 distinction between homologous and analogous proteins gave the field its vocabulary — and its first precise idea, that the event separating two sequences is what matters.

Woese and Fox then did the boldest possible version: compare one molecule, 16S rRNA, across all of life, and redraw the tree into three domains. It established the founding move — sequence comparison as a source of evolutionary truth.

The assumption it left behind: that a carefully chosen molecule speaks for the organism. Genome-scale data killed this — different genes give different trees, which is Chapter 6's whole subject.

1995 – 2001

The genome era

Haemophilus influenzae (1995) was the first free-living organism sequenced. But the comparative discipline starts in 1996, when a second bacterial genome allowed the first genome-scale comparison and the derivation of a “minimal gene set”.

That was the template: take two complete gene inventories, intersect them, interpret the intersection. Yeast, worm, fly and Arabidopsis followed; then two human drafts in 2001.

The assumption it left behind: that a gene inventory is a fact about an organism rather than an output of an annotation pipeline. Chapter 2 is the correction.

the pivot2002 – 2011

Clade scale

Mouse (2002) is where comparison becomes the method rather than an afterthought: conserved sequence between human and mouse identified functional elements that neither genome alone revealed. Hardison's 2003 primer names the discipline.

Then the insight that made it powerful: statistical power for detecting constraint comes from total branch length of the tree, not from any one pairwise identity. Hence phastCons and GERP (2005), 12 Drosophila genomes (2007), 29 mammals (2011). Meanwhile Tettelin's pan-genome (2005) and the Neandertal draft (2010) planted the two ideas that would dominate the next era.

The assumption it left behind: that conserved means functional, and that one reference genome per species suffices.

2012 – now

Pangenome and evidence

ENCODE (2012) forced the field to separate biochemical activity from evolutionary constraint — a fight that is really about what counts as evidence.

Simultaneously the substrate changed again: long reads, then telomere-to-telomere assemblies (2022), then the draft human pangenome reference (2023). The coordinate system stopped being a line and became a graph. Earth BioGenome (2018) and VGP made comparability across thousands of genomes the binding constraint rather than sequencing itself.

The open question: whether machine-learning models trained on sequence can replace alignment-based comparative inference. Chapter 13 argues: not yet, and the evidence for the strong claims is mostly author-run.

4
Why it matters practically

Dating a paper's assumptions from its methods

This is the chapter's payoff for refereeing. Methods carry the era they were designed in, and using one outside its era is a specific, nameable mistake.

If a 2026 paper does this…It is reasoning likeThe era-specific assumption it is importing
Uses reciprocal best BLAST hit for orthology in plants~1996That gene inventories intersect cleanly — pre-dates knowing duplication-rich clades break it (ch 3)
Reports one concatenated tree with bootstraps and no concordance factors~2001That genes share one history — pre-dates the multispecies coalescent becoming standard (ch 6)
Calls an element functional because it is conserved~2005Constraint implies the function you are interested in — the ENCODE fight (ch 12)
Treats one assembly per species as the species~2002That a species has a genome — pre-pangenome (ch 8)
Certifies an assembly with N50~2010Contiguity is quality — pre-dates QV, k-mer completeness, false-duplication metrics (ch 2)
Reports significant dN/dS with no alignment audit or correction~2007That a passing likelihood-ratio test is a finding (ch 9)

The generous reading, which is often correct

None of these are stupid. Each was best practice once, and each persists because it is cheap, familiar, and usually gives an answer that looks reasonable. The referee's job is not to sneer at the method but to ask whether the specific conditions that break it apply here — duplication-rich clade, short internal branches, one individual per species. Often they do not, and the old method is fine.

You have understood Chapter 0 when…

Four things you can do without notes

  1. Name the four shifts and, for each, the assumption it broke.
  2. Explain why 2002 rather than 2001 is the field's real starting point.
  3. Given a method, name roughly the era it was designed for and the condition that breaks it.
  4. Say why statistical power for detecting constraint comes from tree depth rather than pairwise identity.
Self-check — try it before opening

A 2026 manuscript compares two insect genomes, identifies conserved non-coding elements by pairwise alignment at >70% identity, and proposes them as regulatory elements. Which era is this reasoning from, and what is the specific problem?

Answer. This is ~2002 reasoning — pairwise conserved non-coding sequence as a functional screen — and it has two era-specific problems. First, pairwise identity is not constraint: at a single divergence you cannot distinguish sequence conserved by selection from sequence that has simply not had time to diverge. That is exactly why the field moved to phylogenetic methods (phastCons, GERP) that estimate substitution rate against a neutral expectation across many lineages. Second, an identity threshold is not a statistical test — it has no null, so it has no false-positive rate.

The fix is era-appropriate: multiple species spanning enough branch length, a neutral model, and a rate-based statistic. And then, per Chapter 12, constraint still would not establish that the element is an enhancer — that needs an assay.
